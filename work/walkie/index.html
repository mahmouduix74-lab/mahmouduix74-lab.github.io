The questionWhere every trip starts

Where should I eat?

In a city you don't know, the simplest question is the hardest one. The research behind Walkie found five reasons why, and two groups of people paying for them.

ALERT 01Unreliable information

Trustworthy, complete details about local places are hard to find.

ALERT 02Hidden places

Good local spots stay buried behind the same few famous names.

ALERT 03No transparency

People can't tell which reviews are genuine.

ALERT 04Feedback goes nowhere

Customers have no clear channel for what they experienced.

ALERT 05One-sided marketing

Businesses tell their own story; customers barely get a voice.

Stuck on the platformVisitors and travellers

They need a quick recommendation they can trust, in a city they don't know.

Never announcedSmall cafés and restaurants

They can't outspend the big chains, so good places stay unseen.

02
ResearchThree platforms already run this route

We rode the other lines first.

Before designing anything, we studied the three leading review and discovery platforms: what each does well, and what each leaves open for someone in Egypt looking for dinner.

1Platform

Trustpilot

Does well

Five-star reviews, and businesses can reply in public.

Leaves open

Built around companies and products, not places near you.

2Platform

Foursquare

Does well

Location-based discovery, check-ins and tips.

Leaves open

Recommendations aren't tuned to the Egyptian market.

3Platform

Yelp

Does well

Search by place and category, with reviews, hours and directions.

Leaves open

Little presence in Egypt, and little social content.

The missing interchange

None of them joined two lines: places near you in Egypt, and recommendations that learn from you.

Those two became Walkie's core, and the reason for everything after.

03
The ridersThree tickets, three journeys

Everyone on board holds a different ticket.

The analysis defined three roles, each with its own use cases, and turned them into functional requirements before a single screen was drawn.

RiderVisitor · local

The user

  • Signs up and keeps a profile
  • Browses posts and reels; likes, comments, saves and shares
  • Searches nearby or in another city
  • Opens a place with its map and reviews
OperatorCafé · restaurant

The business owner

  • Creates a business profile
  • Adds the location and details
  • Posts and reels that promote the place
  • Reads what customers say
ControlPlatform

The admin

  • A dashboard to manage the platform
  • Supervises content and accounts
  • Keeps the line running

The routeHow the work moved, in Agile cycles

Six stations, one line.

Scroll to drive ⟶

STATION 1

Research

Studied Trustpilot, Foursquare and Yelp; defined the gap, the three roles and the requirements.

STATION 2

Sketch

Imagined the whole system across mobile and web before building anything, then sketched it.

STATION 3

Inspire & systemise

A Figma page of references, and a component library of buttons, cards, tabs, inputs and navigation.

STATION 4

Design

77 mobile screens and the full website flow in Figma, with empty states, errors and accessibility modes.

STATION 5

Build

Five subsystems in parallel, website, app, admin, an AI category filter and the backend, on one REST API.

STATION 6

Iterate

Weekly reviews with the supervisor fed back into the design and the code.

Stop 05 · 1 / 8 · Interests

Personal from the very first minute.

After three short onboarding screens, Walkie asks which city you'll explore, the kind of place you want and your budget, and even how to get to that city.

2 / 8 · Home

A home that knows where you are.

A greeting in your chosen city, then search, friends' stories, gift banners, posts with a location pin, recommended places, top restaurants and what's on.

3 / 8 · Directions

Discovery that ends at the door.

Every place leads to directions three ways: a Google Maps pin, step-by-step bus directions with the travel time, and the metro.

4 / 8 · Reels

Reels for places, not just people.

Nearby, Following and For You. Duets let one visitor answer another, and every reel carries a location pin, so a video becomes a visit.

5 / 8 · Community

A local conversation, not a list of reviews.

Ask a question, share an event or a trip, get suggestions by category, and catch offers such as a discounted tour of the Suez Canal.

6 / 8 · Business profile

Four tabs, in the order you decide.

Directions, Reels, Products, Reviews. Followers and a Message button sit on top, so a visitor can talk to the place directly.

7 / 8 · Gifts

Rewards for real participation.

Spin to win, points for watching, sharing with friends, and four badge tiers: Silver, Gold, Platinum and Diamond.

8 / 8 · Events & trips

From food into local tourism.

Upcoming events and trips, like the Cairo Tower or the Pyramids, with the date, who's going, the full itinerary and Book now.

Step-free accessThe Accessibility Centre · try it

A discovery app usually assumes perfect sight, hearing and hands. Walkie doesn't.

The app's Accessibility Centre opens with one plain question, "What is your problem?", and offers five paths. Pick one below: the demo runs on Walkie's real screens.

Every stateIncluding the quiet stops

Designed for the moments nobody screenshots.

Empty profiles, no comments yet, a forgotten password, a wrong turn. These are the states people hit every day, and the file covers them, along with notifications, share sheets, reporting and chat screens designed ahead of their build.

ArrivalsWhat the line makes possible

Where the line leads.

Walkie was delivered as an academic project, so its value is in what it makes possible, not in live usage numbers.

For visitors

A faster, more trustworthy choice, from discovery to directions.

For small places

Free, authentic visibility through customers' reels and reviews.

For the community

A shared local space for questions, events and trips.

For inclusion

A discovery app people with hearing, sight, motor or reading challenges can use.

Notes from the journey.

  1. Research earns the idea.

    Riding the other lines first showed exactly where Walkie should differ: local to Egypt, and personal.

  2. Accessibility changes the whole design.

    Five different needs forced clearer hierarchy, bigger targets and alternatives to gestures, and those help everyone.

  3. Design the full journey.

    Following people past "here's a place" to "here's how to get there" made Walkie useful, not only informative.

  4. A shared system keeps a team on one line.

    One component library across mobile and web kept seven people and two platforms consistent.

  5. Design every state.

    Empty, error and edge states took real effort, and they are what make a product feel finished.
